## 2.2 Transit network design and route rationalisation

The transit network design and frequency setting problem (TNDFSP) is among the most studied problems in
transport operations research, and three reviews map its shape well: Guihaire and Hao (2008) survey the
integration of network design with scheduling; Ibarra-Rojas et al. (2015) organise the field by planning
horizon from strategic design to real-time control; and Ceder (2007, 2016) provides the canonical
practitioner-facing treatment, in which network design, frequency setting, timetabling, vehicle scheduling and
crew scheduling form a sequential chain. Vuchic (2005) supplies the operational fundamentals that the
downstream stages of the present paper rely on — cycle time, layover, and the relation between headway and
fleet — and the *Transit Capacity and Quality of Service Manual* (TRB, 2013) codifies the service standards
against which any resulting plan is judged.

Across this literature the object being optimised is a *route set with frequencies*, and the optimisation is
almost always bi-objective: minimise generalised passenger cost (in-vehicle time, wait time, transfer
penalties) subject to, or traded against, operator cost. What varies is the solution technique — from
mathematical programming through metaheuristics to hyper-heuristics. What does not vary is the input. The
objective function is defined over passenger flows, so passenger flows must be known.

Route *rationalisation* — restructuring an inherited, organically grown network rather than designing one from
nothing — is the applied sub-case most relevant here, and it is disproportionately represented in consultancy
reporting rather than in the peer-reviewed record. This asymmetry matters for the gap argument in §2.8:
rationalisation is what authorities actually commission, and it is the stage for which the published
methodological guidance is thinnest.

## 2.3 Demand estimation and its data requirements

Where demand is not directly observed, the standard recourse is to estimate it — the four-step model, or in
modern practice an activity-based or a direct-demand model. All of these require calibration data: a household
travel survey, a fare-card or smart-card series, automatic passenger counts, or at minimum boarding counts on
a sample of routes. The data-requirement question is therefore not whether demand can be modelled, but what
the modelling itself consumes.

This is where secondary Indian cities fail the precondition rather than fail the method. Badami and Haider
(2007) document the institutional and financial weakness of Indian urban bus provision, and Pucher et al.
(2007) characterise the resulting service crisis; neither describes a data environment in which a household
OD survey is routinely available. The practical consequence, set out in §1.3, is a deadlock: the authority
cannot optimise without demand data, and cannot fund demand data without first demonstrating that optimisation
would pay.

## 2.4 Demand-independent and accessibility-based approaches

A second tradition sidesteps demand entirely by planning against *accessibility* — the opportunities a
resident can reach — rather than against realised trips. Accessibility-based planning changes the question
from "where do people travel?" to "where could people travel?", and its inputs are supply-side: population,
opportunity locations, and a network.

The catchment measurement literature is the part of this tradition the present paper depends on most
directly, and it is also the part most often applied carelessly. Untermann (1984) established the ~400 m
walking norm that almost every transit catchment study still uses. El-Geneidy et al. (2014) showed with
observed data that real walking distances to transit are longer and more variable than the norm assumes.
Most importantly for the method in §4, **Gutiérrez and García-Palomares (2008) and Biba, Curtin and Manca
(2010) had already demonstrated, more than fifteen years ago, that Euclidean buffers overstate transit
catchment populations relative to network-based measures**, and Biba et al. proposed a parcel-level network
method precisely to correct it.

## 2.5 Open data and planning under scarcity

The feasibility of the supply-side route depends on whether open data are now good enough to carry it. Three
strands say they are, with caveats. Gridded population products — WorldPop (Tatem, 2017) chief among them —
provide modelled population at 100 m resolution globally, at the cost of being a *model* anchored on an older
census rather than an observation. OpenStreetMap has reached usable completeness for road networks in much of
the world; Barrington-Leigh and Millard-Ball (2017) estimate global road-network completeness at about 83%
and, critically for this paper, find completeness is not uniform — it is lower in exactly the peripheral,
lower-density areas where service gaps are most likely. Open routing engines, notably OSRM (Luxen and Vetter,
2011), make continental-scale travel-time queries free at the point of use.

A parallel and more directly comparable body of work digitises informal and paratransit networks in the Global
South — the Digital Matatus project in Nairobi being the exemplar — and demonstrates that a usable network
representation can be built where no agency holds one. That literature, however, largely stops at
*representation*: it produces a map and a GTFS feed, not a rationalised plan with frequencies and a fleet.

## 2.6 Network indicators and equity assessment

Two methodological toolkits recur in the evaluation half of this paper. For classification, Jenks (1967)
natural breaks remains the standard for partitioning a continuous planning index into service tiers, with
goodness-of-variance-fit as the selection criterion and Cohen's (1960) κ as the agreement statistic against
alternative partitions. For weighting a composite index without expert input, entropy weighting derived from
Shannon (1948) and principal components (Jolliffe, 2002) provide data-driven alternatives to asserted weights.
For distributional evaluation, Delbosc and Currie (2011) established the Gini/Lorenz apparatus for transport
equity, and Moran's (1950) *I* tests whether a residual — here, uncovered population — is spatially clustered
or random, which is the difference between a policy-actionable gap and noise.

## 2.7 Indian and Global South evidence

The Indian evidence base is strongest on diagnosis and weakest on method. Badami and Haider (2007) and Pucher
et al. (2007) establish the provision deficit; MoHUA's service-level benchmarks (2014) establish the norm
against which a plan is judged, including the buses-per-1,000 target used in §5.7; and the CSE & CITIES Forum
(2026) assessment quantifies how far short current provision falls. What is largely missing is a published,
reproducible *method* by which an Indian secondary-city authority without ridership data can get from an
inherited permit register to a costed service plan. That absence is the gap this paper addresses.

## 2.8 Synthesis: the gap, quantified

The synthesis matrix (**Table 1**) cross-tabulates two of its coded columns: **data intensity (1–5)** against
**"produces an implementable plan (yes/no)"**. The expectation the corpus should be tested against is that
implementable plans cluster at data intensity 4–5, while open-data studies cluster at "no implementable
plan" — that is, the literature can either plan with rich data, or describe with poor data, but rarely plans
with poor data. Figure 2 renders that cross-tabulation as a shaded gap region.

> publishable finding, and a gap asserted without the table behind it is not.

The analytical wedge that follows from this is the intellectual hinge of the manuscript, and it is worth
stating flatly. **Fleet sizing and coverage analysis never required demand data in the first place.** Cycle
time is a function of distance, speed and dwell; fleet is a function of cycle time and headway; coverage is a
function of geometry and population. Demand enters the conventional chain at exactly one point — the frequency
rule, which sets headway from peak link load. Replace that one rule with a *policy-anchored* headway band
driven by an ordinal supply-side index, and every downstream quantity in the chain becomes computable from
open data alone. The contribution of this paper is not a claim that the index predicts ridership; it is the
demonstration that severing the chain at that single point yields decisions that are robust to the index's
uncertainty.
